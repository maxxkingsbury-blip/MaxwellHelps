The basics

The four parts of Medicare

Medicare is federal health insurance for people 65 and older, and for some younger people with certain disabilities or conditions. It is split into four lettered parts, each covering something different.

A

Part A: Hospital insurance

Inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice and some home health care. Most people pay no premium if they or a spouse worked and paid Medicare taxes for about 10 years.

B

Part B: Medical insurance

Doctor visits, outpatient care, lab work, medical equipment and many preventive services. Part B has a monthly premium, which is higher for people with higher incomes.

C

Part C: Medicare Advantage

Private plans approved by Medicare that bundle Parts A and B, and usually D, into one plan. Many include extras such as dental, vision and hearing.

D

Part D: Prescription drugs

Drug coverage offered by private plans. In 2026, what you pay out of pocket for covered drugs is capped at $2,100 for the year.

How the pieces fit

Two paths to your coverage

Everyone starts with Original Medicare (Parts A and B) from the federal government. From there, most people choose one of two paths. Neither is right for everyone. The best fit depends on your health, your doctors, your prescriptions, how much you travel and your budget.

Path 1Original Medicare + Medigap + Part D

Keep Original Medicare, add a Medicare Supplement (Medigap) plan to help with costs Medicare doesn't pay, and add a standalone Part D drug plan.

  • See any doctor nationwide who accepts Medicare
  • More predictable out-of-pocket costs
  • Usually a higher monthly premium
OR

Path 2Medicare Advantage (Part C)

Choose one private plan that replaces how you get your Part A and B benefits, and usually includes drug coverage.

  • Often a low or $0 plan premium
  • Uses a provider network, with copays as you go
  • Yearly limit on in-network out-of-pocket costs; many include dental, vision and hearing

You keep paying your Part B premium on either path. A $0-premium plan still has copays, coinsurance and deductibles.

Timing matters

Key enrollment dates

Enrolling on time is as important as choosing the right plan. Missing your window without other qualifying coverage can mean late-enrollment penalties that last as long as you have Medicare.

Initial Enrollment Period

A 7-month window around your 65th birthday: the 3 months before, your birthday month, and the 3 months after.

Annual Enrollment · Oct 15 – Dec 7

Switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, change plans, or join, drop or switch Part D. Changes start January 1.

MA Open Enrollment · Jan 1 – Mar 31

If you're in a Medicare Advantage plan, you can switch to another one, or return to Original Medicare and add Part D.

Special Enrollment Periods

Certain life events, such as moving or losing employer coverage, can open a window to make changes outside the usual dates.

Your Medigap open enrollment period is the 6 months starting the month you're 65 or older and enrolled in Part B. It's usually the easiest time to buy a Medigap plan.

Getting started

Common first questions

Is Medicare free?

Not entirely. Part A is premium-free for most people, but Part B has a monthly premium, and Part D and Medicare Advantage premiums vary by plan. Even a $0-premium plan has copays and deductibles.

Is Medicare the same as Medicaid?

No. Medicare is federal insurance based mainly on age or disability, regardless of income. Medicaid is a state and federal program for people with limited income and resources. Some people qualify for both.

When should I start learning about Medicare?

About six months before you turn 65. That gives you time to understand your options before your Initial Enrollment Period opens.

Do I need Part D if I don't take any medications?

If you don't have other creditable drug coverage, enrolling when you're first eligible helps you avoid a Part D late-enrollment penalty, which is added to your premium for as long as you have drug coverage.

What if I'm still working at 65?

It depends on your employer coverage and the size of your employer. Some people can delay Part B without a penalty. Talk with us or your benefits administrator before you decide.
